The Liveness Problem

The protocol we've described so far is safe. All nodes agree on decisions. But there's a problem: it doesn't guarantee that anything actually decides.

It's possible to construct a scenario where the system gets stuck. Two leaders keep competing, and neither gets a decision through. This is the liveness problem.

The ping-pong scenario

Imagine two leaders, λ and λ'. Both want to get value v proposed for slot 1. They keep fighting:

  • λ runs a scout in ballot (0, λ), gets a majority to adopt it.
  • λ' runs a scout in ballot (0, λ'), gets a majority to adopt it (because (0, λ') > (0, λ)).
  • Now λ can't get acceptors in the majority to accept its value—they've adopted λ's higher ballot.
  • λ tries a higher ballot (1, λ). Gets a majority to adopt it.
  • λ' tries (1, λ'), gets a majority to adopt it.
  • And on and on...

Neither leader completes. No value is chosen. The system is safe but stuck.

Why does this happen?

The basic protocol has no built-in way to prevent leaders from constantly escalating ballots. Both leaders are acting correctly according to the protocol. But they're competing without coordination.

The FLP impossibility result (Fischer, Lynch, Paterson) tells us something important: in an asynchronous system with crashes, no protocol can guarantee both safety and liveness without additional assumptions.

Paxos is safe without any assumptions. But to get liveness, we need something extra: a way to prevent unbounded ballot escalation. We need a leader.

Why one leader solves it

If only one leader is active at a time, the ping-pong never happens. That leader runs scouts and commanders without competition. Values get chosen. The system makes progress.

So the solution is to elect a leader. But in an asynchronous system, you can't really "elect" (that's an impossibility). Instead, Paxos uses a weaker concept called weak leader election, which we'll see next.
