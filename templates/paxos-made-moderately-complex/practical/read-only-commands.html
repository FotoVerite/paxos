Read-Only Commands

Paxos treats all commands the same: they all go through consensus. This is safe, but it's inefficient. Read-only commands don't change state, so they don't need to coordinate across the cluster.

But there's a catch: a client might query a stale replica (one that's behind in the log). You have to handle this.

The naive approach and why it fails

The naive approach: a client sends a read to any replica, and the replica answers from its current state.

The problem: if that replica is behind (hasn't received all decisions yet), the answer might be stale. The client sees state from 5 seconds ago, not the current state.

This violates consistency guarantees. Clients expect to see consistent snapshots of the system.

Approach 1: Run through consensus

The safe approach: treat read-only commands like write commands. Propose them through Synod, get a decision, then execute them. Once all nodes agree on when the read happens, it's safe.

The downside: this is slow. You're doing full consensus just to read.

Approach 2: Leases

A better approach is leases. The leader (the active ballot holder) gets a "lease" from acceptors: "I'm the current leader, and no one will take over for the next 10 seconds."

With a lease, the leader can serve read-only commands directly from its state, trusting that it's up-to-date. Other replicas that are running the active leader's commands can also serve reads.

But this requires a timing assumption: you must have a known bound on clock drift. In an asynchronous system, leases don't work.

Approach 3: Quorum reads

Another approach: read from a quorum. Ask a majority of replicas for their current state and take the one that's furthest ahead in the log.

This guarantees consistency without needing consensus or timing assumptions. But it's slower than reading from a single replica.

The Van Renesse recommendation

Leases are practical and widely used. They require a timing assumption, but in most real networks, clock drift bounds are reasonable. Leases give you both fast reads and consistency.

The exact details depend on your system's assumptions and requirements.
