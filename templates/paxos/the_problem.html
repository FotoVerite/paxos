Section 1. The problem

Requirements

When confronted with the complexity of a problem, logically one would state in clear terms the issues one is trying to solve. Lamport opted to dress up the problem in allgory and obscuating language.

The requirements of the parliamentary protocol could be achieved only by providing the legislators with the necessary resources. Each legislator received a sturdy ledger in which to record the decrees, a pen, and a supply of indelible ink. Legislators might forget what they had been doing if they left the Chamber,3 so they would write notes in the back of the ledgers to remind themselves of important parliamentary tasks. An entry in the list of decrees was never changed, but notes could be crossed out. Achieving the progress condition required that legislators be able to measure the passage of time, so they were given simple hourglass timers. Legislators carried their ledgers at all times, and could always read the list of decrees and any note that had not been crossed out. The ledgers were made of the finest parchment and were used for only the most important notes. A legislator would write other notes on a slip of paper, which he might (or might not) lose if he left the Chamber. The acoustics of the Chamber were poor, making oratory impossible. Legislators could communicate only by messenger, and were provided with funds to hire as many messengers as they needed. A messenger could be counted on not to garble messages, but he might forget that he had already delivered a message, and deliver it again. Like the legislators they served, messengers devoted only part of their time to parliamentary duties. A messenger might leave the Chamber to conduct some business—perhaps taking a six-month voyage—before delivering a message. He might even leave forever, in which case the message would never be delivered. Although legislators and messengers could enter and leave at any time, when inside the Chamber they devoted themselves to the business of Parliament. While they remained in the Chamber, messengers delivered messages in a timely fashion and legislators reacted promptly to any messages they received.

When stripped of the storytelling, what he is trying to illustrate is the issues of values being agreed to by nodes in a system. In a long-running distributed system with multiple nodes, failures and message delays are inevitable. This is especially true with systems at scale that include thousands of nodes; even a 0.0001% failure rate will manifest over time.

This is what Lamport is trying to illustrate with legislators coming and going, they are the nodes liveliness. This is a the main constraint he will wrestle with.

"The problem of governing with a part-time parliament bears a remarkable correspondence to the problem faced by today’s fault-tolerant distributed systems, where legislators correspond to processes and leaving the Chamber corresponds to failing"

If a legislator is a node, what is a decree? What is a ledger.

Part of the reason for the metaphor is he wants it to be a bit elastic what they can be. The decree is a discreet value that is agreed to, the ledger is some way of it being recorded that stable. It could be memory, it could be a database, it could be json document. That part isn't important to Lamport. He is more interested in the process of how they are agreed to and recorded. He also is making concrete the requirement that each node owns it's own recording and that it isn't a shared between them. So a database they were all using as their main ledger would not count not would it truly be distributed then, that would be centralized.

Any of the other metadata, the "notes in the margins" cannot be counted on to always be there and accessible. Messages, IE messengers might never be delivered or delayed. Time must be kept locally at least in a simplistic manner, IE the hourglass timers. And there must be a retry mechanism, what those are are outside the main scope of the paper.

The other main requirement he is concerned with is consistency. All legislators much agree. Agreement doesn't not mean the same, it means non contradictory. They can contain different decrees but for any decree that overlap they must have the same value

One requirement he is not concerned with is disagreements among the legislators. He is assuming they all trust each other, no Byzantine faults, no malicious actors, and no clients trying to propose different values for the same decrees. He is basically saying transactions are not an problem this is to address and is outside the scope.

In modern parliaments, the passing of decrees is hindered by disagreement among legislators. This was not the case in Paxos, where an atmosphere of mutual trust prevailed
